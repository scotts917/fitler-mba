The Toolkit · 1 of 7 · Profitability

Is the work itself making money?

Gross Margin (revenue − cost of goods sold) ÷ revenue
Of each revenue dollar, how much survives the direct cost of delivering?
How to read it: this is the money left to pay for everything else — marketing, salaries, rent, you. Higher means more cushion. When a company sells more than one thing, compute it per product line; a blended number hides the weak half.
Net Margin net income ÷ revenue
After absolutely everything — did the year make money?
How to read it: the final verdict per dollar of sales. Negative isn't automatically fatal — but it always demands the next question: what is funding the gap, and for how long?
The same gauge reads very differently by business model
IndustryTypical gross marginTypical net margin
Software / SaaS70–85%often negative while growing
Professional services30–50%5–15%
Healthcare providers30–45%2–8%
Supermarkets25–30%1–3%
Bankingno COGS — uses net interest margin instead20–30%

Read this table as intuition, not as a benchmark. A supermarket at 27% is thriving; a software company at 27% is in trouble. "Healthy" is a fact about a business model — and the only comparison that truly binds is your own history.

The Toolkit · 2 of 7 · Cash

How fast is cash leaving — and how long until it's gone?

Burn Rate cash out − cash in, per month or quarter
How much cash does the business consume per period?
How to read it: profit is an opinion shaped by accounting rules; burn is a fact shaped by your bank balance. A profitable company can burn cash — and an unprofitable one can generate it. Burn is measured from the cash flow statement, never from the P&L.
Runway cash on hand ÷ burn per period
At this burn rate, how long does the business live?
How to read it: the most consequential division in a founder's life. Under twelve months and fixing it becomes the day job — because raising money takes about six, and desperation is visible from across the table.

A worked example. A company holds $600K in the bank and spends $50K more than it collects each month. Runway = 600 ÷ 50 = 12 months. Cut the burn to $40K and runway becomes 15 months — the same three months you'd have bought with a $150K raise, and you keep the equity.

The Toolkit · 3 of 7 · Liquidity

Can you cover what's due soon?

Current Ratio current assets ÷ current liabilities
Do near-term resources cover near-term obligations?
  • Below 1.0 — bills coming due exceed resources available. A flashing light.
  • Around 2.0 — conventionally comfortable for most operating businesses.
  • Very high — not automatically good; can mean cash sitting idle or inventory piling up.
A caution about this gauge

The formula adds three very different things together and treats them as interchangeable:

  • Cash — spendable today.
  • Receivables — spendable when customers actually pay.
  • Inventory — spendable only if it sells, and only at whatever price it then fetches.

A current ratio of 2.0 built on cash means something very different from a 2.0 built on unsold product. Remember this — it matters tonight.

Gauge 5 · Current ratio
The Toolkit · 4 of 7 · Inventory Speed

Is product moving — or sitting?

Inventory Turnover cost of goods sold ÷ inventory
How many times a year does the shelf empty and refill?
Why COGS on top, not revenue: inventory sits on the books at what you paid for it. Dividing a selling-price number by a cost number would smuggle your markup into the answer. Cost over cost — always.
Days Sales of Inventory 365 ÷ turnover
The same gauge with a friendlier face: how long does a unit sit before it sells?
Turnover and DSI are one measurement stated two ways. 6 turns = 61 days. 4 turns = 91 days. 2.4 turns = 152 days. Days are easier to feel: that box has been in the warehouse since March.
Worked example · a coffee roaster
Cost of goods sold, full year: $1,200,000
Inventory on the shelf: $250,000
Turns = 1,200,000 ÷ 250,000 = 4.8×
Days = 365 ÷ 4.8 = 76 days

The average bag sits about two and a half months before it sells.

Two footnotes worth saying out loud
  • Ending vs. average inventory. Textbooks divide by average inventory — beginning plus ending, over two. With a single balance sheet in front of you, ending inventory is the practical choice. Tonight we use ending inventory. In a fast-growing year the two answers can differ a lot, so always say which one you used.
  • Match the numerator to the segment. If only part of the business carries inventory, use that segment's COGS — not the whole company's.

The Toolkit · 5 of 7 · The Inventory Drag

Inventory is cash wearing a costume — and the costume costs money.

Not a ratio — a discipline. Every dollar on that shelf is a dollar that can't make payroll, and while it sits, it quietly bills you five ways:

  • Capital tied up — cash already paid, frozen until sale
  • Storage & handling — warehouses, freight, the moving of boxes
  • Insurance & shrink — protecting it, losing some anyway
  • Obsolescence — the world moves; the box doesn't
  • Forced discounting — when it finally must move, it moves at a markdown

The first three accrue quietly, every month. The last two arrive all at once as a write-down — the accounting word for admitting the shelf was a bonfire. Watch for it in the story.

The Toolkit · 6 of 7 · Service Businesses

No inventory? Then your receivables are your inventory.

Days Sales Outstanding accounts receivable ÷ revenue × 365
How long after doing the work do you actually get paid?
Our example business: $310K of receivables on $2.4M of revenue = 47 days. Seven weeks of completed work sitting unpaid — the exact structural twin of five months of product on a shelf.
The Receivables Drag the service business's carrying cost
  • Capital tied up — you delivered, you paid your people, and now you are financing your client
  • Collection labor — someone's hours spent chasing invoices instead of selling
  • Early-pay discounts — the services version of a markdown
  • Bad debt — the services version of obsolescence: work delivered, never paid for

Why the aging matters more than the total. DSO gives you one number; the aging tells you whether that number is healthy or hiding something. The Toolkit · 7 of 7 · Leverage

How much of this business was built with borrowed money?

Debt-to-Equity total debt ÷ stockholders' equity
Whose money is at risk here — yours, or the bank's?
How to read it: leverage is an amplifier. It multiplies your returns in a good year and your losses in a bad one, and it never asks which kind of year you're having.
The same $600K business, financed three ways

One standing rule for every gauge in this toolkit: when a ratio returns a bizarre answer, don't excuse it and don't average it away — take it apart and find out which component is lying. Tonight ends on a spectacular example.

Chapter One · A Financing Interlude

February 2021: Wall Street lends Peloton a billion dollars. For free.

First, the instrument — new to most rooms

A convertible note is a loan with a lottery ticket stapled to it. The lender can be repaid like any normal loan — or convert the IOU into stock at a price fixed on day one. That fixed price is the conversion price, and it works exactly like a strike price: worthless if the stock stays below it, valuable the moment the stock climbs above. Because the ticket has value, lenders accept less interest in exchange. Sometimes far less.

What the lender collects in February 2026, at any share price
The terms
Amount borrowed$1.0B
Coupon0%
Conversion price$239.23
per $1,000 note≈ 4.18 shares
Share price at pricing$144.99
Premium to market65%
MaturityFebruary 2026

Translate the terms: sophisticated institutions charged nothing to lend a money-losing company a billion dollars, because they were confident the stock would climb another 65% from an all-time high. That is how loudly the headlines were speaking in February 2021. File one detail away: these notes come due in five years.

The Story · Chapter Three
Peloton Today
How Did They Survive?
  • They stopped building bikes. Owned manufacturing exited; production handed to partners. No factories, no fixed cost.
  • They shrank the shelf. Inventory fell from $1.54B at the peak to $206M — an 87% reduction.
  • They let the good business carry them. Subscription now generates the overwhelming majority of gross profit.
  • Result: blended gross margin recovered from 19.5% to roughly 51%, and recent quarters have turned profitable.

Chapter Three · The Lottery Ticket

The bet, drawn to scale.

Read the gold line first. To convert, the stock had to clear $239.23. It had never traded there — not even at the December 2020 peak of about $152. Lenders weren't betting on a recovery; they were betting the best month in company history was a waypoint.

Now read the white line. From the day the notes priced, it went one direction. By the maturity date in February 2026 the stock was in single digits — roughly 97% below the conversion price. The ticket was never worth anything, so the loan was always just a loan.

Chapter Three · The Interlude Comes Due

Debt is a promise made by your most optimistic self.

The life of one billion dollars
WhenWhat happenedCost of that money
Feb 2021$1.0B borrowed. Lenders take stock upside instead of interest; strike set at $239.23 with the shares at $144.99.0%
2022–2025The shares never approach the strike. The lottery ticket quietly expires. The loan remains a loan — full principal, fixed due date.0%
Feb 2026Notes mature with the stock in single digits. Nobody converts. $1.0B repaid in cash by a company with negative equity — refinanced in part with $350M of notes due 2029.5.50%

The lesson in one line: a 0% loan was never free money. It was a bet that the stock would be worth more than the debt. When the bet lost, the "free" billion became an ordinary billion-dollar obligation — due on a date chosen by the version of the company that had just had its best year ever.

Why this is your problem too: the same shape appears at every scale — the equipment loan taken in the best quarter, the lease signed at peak demand, the line of credit drawn when orders were pouring in. Borrowed money is repaid by whoever you turn out to be on the due date, not by the person who signed.

Cost of capital is a gauge too. Zero to five and a half is the market restating its opinion of you — in basis points.

Before We Build

Important note: different size companies have different metrics.

The eight gauges are universal. The numbers you compare them against are not — and three things change when the company is yours rather than public.

  • Normalize your own paycheck. In a public company, executive pay is a market number. In yours, owner compensation is a tax and lifestyle decision wearing an expense's clothes. Restate it at what you'd pay a stranger to do the job before you trust any margin.
  • Expect small-N volatility. Peloton's gauges move on millions of transactions; yours move when one $80K invoice lands a week late. A single month means almost nothing — read trends across quarters.
  • Never borrow another company's benchmarks. Scale changes the arithmetic: a public company's 12% net margin and your 12% net margin describe entirely different businesses with different cost structures, tax treatment, and access to capital. Your own history is the comparison that binds.
Carpenter Hall, for sixty seconds

Her 44% gross margin — strong or weak? Unanswerable until you know it's a service business with her own hours priced in. Her deferred revenue is Peloton's subscription liability in miniature: cash collected, work still owed. And for her the inventory gauges retire entirely — DSO takes their seat. The dialect changes; the grammar doesn't.

After the break

You just read a company backward.
Now build one forward.

Everything tonight was diagnosis — statements someone else already lived. A pro forma is the same statements written before they happen. And its first reader won't be an accountant. It'll be an angel investor, who reads four numbers first:

  • When do you die? — the monthly cash line
  • How much do you need? — the capital requirement
  • When does this stop needing you? — the break-even month
  • Is the work itself profitable? — gross margin, gauge 1, again

Deliberately parked for the fundraising sessions: NPV, IRR, DCF, and valuation. Tonight is survival math, not deal math.
