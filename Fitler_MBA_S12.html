Fitler MBA · S12 · Part 2

How a VC fund works

Predict: "Whose money is the VC investing?" Pays off S11 slide 4: the angel invests their own money, the VC invests other people's.

LPs don't wire the money on day one. They commit, and the GP calls it as deals come up. Cash comes back up the same chain when companies sell.

For a pension fund, VC is a small slice. They're buying the chance at outsized returns, and they're picky about managers. Top quartile or nothing.

The GP is running a business. Its customers are the LPs. It goes back to them every 3 to 4 years for the next fund, before the last one has shown much cash. That's why early markups matter so much to a VC.

Click: the clock. New companies in the first 3 to 4 years. Reserves go into the winners for years after that. Exits and cash back mostly come in the back half. Ten years, often extended a year or two.

The J-curve. LPs are in a hole for years: fees and losses show up early, wins show up late. Same $100M fund as the next slide: LPs put in $98M and end up $196M ahead. The real version of this, by vintage, comes later tonight.

Why all MBAs want to be VCs

Story slot: the fund side of the table. Why you're here today: 2/20.

The fee keeps the lights on. The carry is where partners get rich, and carry only comes from profits.

Click 1, the target. 25% IRR. Ask first: "25% a year for 7 years. What multiple is that?" 4.8x. Then the catch: the money doesn't all go in on day one or come out on the last day. The average dollar is out about 5 years, so 25% works out to about 3x for LPs. Callback S06 IRR.

Click 2, the sample fund. $100M committed, $14M of fees, $86M goes to work, $350M comes back. Where the $350M comes from is the fund dynamics slide in two minutes.

Predict before click 3: "The LPs put in $98M. What do they get back? What does the GP take home on its $2M?"

LPs $294M, 3.0x: right on the target. GP $56M, 28x, and $50M of it is carry on other people's money. That's why every MBA wants to be a VC.

Story slot: Cross Atlantic, 1999. GP commit was 5% then. Today's median is 1.7% (Carta 2025). An 8% hurdle was common too; today only about 1 in 10 VC funds has one.

Say out loud: simplified. Fees usually step down after the investment period, and this is a whole-fund waterfall: LPs get all their money back before any carry. Some funds pay carry deal by deal, with a clawback.

The stage ladder

Two definitions first: post-money, and sold in the round.

Spoken over the table, the question each stage asks: Can it work? (pre-seed, angels). Is it working? (seed, A). Will it scale? (B and up).

Each round is priced at roughly 2 to 3 times the last. That's the growth an investor needs to see to mark you up.

Predict before the click: "The seed fund buys 18%. If it never puts in another dollar, what does it own at a Series D exit?"

About 11%. Our 15% before the break was generous. Callback to reserves on the fund dynamics slide: follow-on checks defend the 18%.

Who you'll actually meet: partner, principal, associate. An associate can't say yes. Ask who decides, and how.

Why a VC passes on Éclat

Predict: "Would First Round write this check?"

Walk the three outcomes. A $20M sale would be a great result for a craft chocolatier: 7.2x and about a 33% IRR. That's a terrific angel investment.

Click: the VC row. The fund needs one company to return about 66x, roughly an 82% IRR for seven years. Éclat's best case is $3.6M, about 1% of the $285M the winner has to deliver. And the realistic cases are 9 to 20%, which is index-fund territory. Callback slide 9.

It isn't the IRR alone. It's the ceiling. A VC can live with most deals going to zero. It can't live with a deal whose best case can't move the fund.
