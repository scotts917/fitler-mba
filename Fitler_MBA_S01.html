Frame 01
First principle

A startup is a temporary thing.

It exists to find something. Once it finds it, the startup ends — and the company begins. If you confuse the two, you'll run the wrong playbook.

"A startup is a temporary organization designed to search for a repeatable and scalable business model."

Frame 02
Two different muscles

Search vs. execute.

Steve Blank's Customer Development model · the spine of Lean Startup
Search · Startup mode

Finding the unknown

  • Hypotheses, not plans
  • Customer interviews, not customer support
  • Throw it out and try again
  • Measured in learnings per week
  • Founder-led, ambiguity-tolerant
  • "Are we onto something?"
Execute · Company mode

Scaling the known

  • Plans, budgets, OKRs
  • Process, repeatability, predictability
  • Optimize the engine you've got
  • Measured in revenue, retention, margin
  • Management-led, ambiguity-averse
  • "Are we hitting the number?"

Frame 04
The hardest part isn't learning

The corporate unlearn.

You didn't get to where you are by accident. The reflexes that made you successful in a big org will quietly sabotage you in a startup. Four to swap, starting tonight.

i. Build consensus before deciding → Make the call with 70% of the info
ii. Polish before shipping → Ship the ugly version, learn faster
iii. Protect the downside → Optimize for the upside
iv. Headcount equals power → Leverage equals power

Frame 05
Andy Kessler · Eat People

Five rules from Kessler.

Andy Kessler ran a hedge fund and wrote one of the most useful "how the world actually works" books for entrepreneurs. The whole book is ten rules. These five are the ones that change how you see an opportunity.

i. If it doesn't scale, it'll go stale. Boutiques are lovely; they don't compound. Look for things that can grow without breaking.
ii. Waste what's abundant to make up for what's scarce. Compute is cheap. Attention is expensive. Spend the cheap thing freely to save the dear one.
iii. Intelligence moves to the edge of the network. Power flows away from centers, toward users. Build for the edge, not the hub.
iv. Embrace exceptionalism. Average is the enemy. Find the outlier customers, the outlier markets, the outlier you.
v. Eat people. Productivity replaces labor. The rule sounds harsh; the dynamic is unstoppable. Be on the right side of it.
Selected from Eat People — Andy Kessler (2011)
Read the book. The other five rules are also good07 / 19
The hunt
Idea generation, four sources

Where ideas come from.

Not from staring at a blank page. Ideas live in places — you have to know where to look. Four sources, in roughly the order of how often they pay off.

i.

Solve a problem you have.

The frustration you've ranted about three times this month. The hack you built for yourself. The thing you'd pay someone to fix. Founder-market fit starts here — you already understand the pain, the language, the workaround, the urgency.

Highest hit rate
ii.

Observe and research.

Watch where people waste time. Read trade publications outside your field. Listen for "I wish there was a way to..." Pain points hide in plain sight when you're looking for them — most people stopped noticing years ago.

Patience required
iii.

Have a conversation with Claude.

Tell it about your experience, your skills, your network. Ask what problems are adjacent. Ask what's underserved. Use it as a thinking partner, not an answer machine — your job is to push back on whatever it suggests.

New muscle
iv.

Pick a user group and investigate.

"Aging parents." "Independent contractors." "Solo lawyers." Pick a tribe you care about. Go deep on their reality. The problems will introduce themselves once you've spent real time inside the world.

The hero framework
The Four Foundation Questions · Use these all year

The four questions.

If you can't answer all four, you don't have a business — you have a feeling.
01.

What problem do you solve?

Specific, not abstract. "People are lonely" is not a problem. "New retirees lose their daily structure within 90 days of stopping work" is. The sharper the framing, the more obvious the test.

02.

Who's got it?

Name the segment. Estimate the size. If you say "everyone," you're hand-waving. Sharp tribes beat broad markets every single time — narrow your way to clarity.

03.

What's the impact of the problem today?

Urgency and pain magnitude. Is it costing them money? Time? Relationships? Sleep? The more vivid the answer, the better the opportunity. "Mildly annoying" is a no.

04.

Who's willing to pay to make it go away?

Sometimes the payer isn't the user. Sometimes the user is broke. The opportunity is where the pain meets a budget — that intersection is where businesses live.

Strategic posture
Steve Blank · Market entry conditions

Five ways to enter.

Before you know your customers, you should know your market posture. Each of these is a different game, with different risks, different timelines, and different definitions of winning. Pick consciously.

i.

New product,
new market

You're inventing the category. Highest risk, highest reward. You'll spend years educating customers before they know they need you.

SpaceX · Anthropic
ii.

New product,
existing market

Customers already buy something here. You're offering them a better way. Faster path to revenue, but you'd better actually be better.

Tesla · Stripe
iii.

Resegment an existing market

The market exists, but you're carving out a sharper slice — a niche, a price point, a use case nobody serves well. Often the smartest play.

Lululemon · Liquid Death
iv.

New low-cost provider

Same product class, dramatically cheaper. Forces operational excellence. Works when the incumbents have gotten lazy or expensive.

Vanguard · Costco
v.

Geographic replication

A model proven in one place, brought somewhere new. Lower-risk because the model works — but local execution is everything.

Uber · Airbnb (early)

The daily habit
The practice — start tomorrow morning

One yellow pad.

If you only take one thing from tonight, take this. Buy an 8.5 × 14 yellow legal pad. Put it where you start your day. Then run this protocol for six weeks. The results will surprise you.

i.

Capture, don't think.

One-line problem or idea description. No editing, no judging, no "is this any good." Speed beats quality at this stage.

ii.

Commit to five a day.

Five new entries every single day. Some days they'll be brilliant. Most days they'll be junk. That's the point — you can't tell from inside the moment.

iii.

Once a week, review.

Sit down on Sunday. Read everything you wrote. Star the ones that still feel alive. Ignore the others — no shame, just data.

iv.

Page full? Rank.

When you fill a page, score your starred items against the Four Foundation Questions. The top three move to the next page. Everything else stays as compost.

The debrief
Open discussion · 15 minutes

What did the forge reveal?

Not a presentation round — a conversation. I'll throw questions at the room. Anyone can answer. Volunteers welcome, calling on you also welcome. The goal is to make the experience visible to everyone.

i. Which Foundation Question caught you? Where did the sharpening questions force you to admit you didn't actually know the answer? That's the gap to investigate.
ii. What's the lowest score you got — and was it fair? Be honest. Did Claude catch real hand-waving, or did it miss something specific to your context? Both are useful to surface.
iii. Does your sharpened thesis still feel like yours? Sometimes the forge produces a tighter version. Sometimes it pushes you somewhere you didn't intend. What happened to your idea between input and output?
iv. What's the first move on your brief — and will you actually do it? The brief is useless if it sits on your desktop. What's the one task you're committing to before Friday? Who in this room can help?
