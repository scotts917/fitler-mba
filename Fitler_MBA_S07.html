The 4 Foundation Questions

1
What problem do you solve?

Named plainly — not your product, the problem.

2
Who's got it?

Groups you can describe, find, and call.

3
What's the impact today?

What it costs them right now — money, hours, risk.

4
Who pays to make it go away?

Not who likes it. Who pays.

Your answers to these are guesses. An MVP is a machine for testing them — one at a time, cheapest first. Question 4 is where tonight ends.

Act I · What an MVP Buys You

Four words people use interchangeably. They aren't.

Prototype

Answers: can it work? A test of feasibility, shown to yourselves. Nobody buys a prototype.

MVP

Answers: will they use it — will they pay? The smallest thing you can put in front of a real customer that produces a real decision. Tonight's word.

Pilot

Answers: does it survive contact with one real customer? Paid, scoped, delivered for real — the service business's natural MVP. More in Act II.

MLP

Minimum Lovable Product — answers: will they tell anyone? Enough polish that early users become your marketing. A later stage, not a starting point.

Zappos, 1999 — "people will buy shoes online."

Nick Swinmurn can't find the boots he wants at the mall. He believes people would buy shoes on the internet — in 1999, when everyone knows you'd never buy shoes you can't try on. What does he build?

He builds almost nothing. He walks into local shoe stores, photographs the inventory, and posts the photos on a bare-bones site. When an order comes in, he goes back to the store, buys the pair at full retail, and ships it himself — losing money on every sale.

What it cost
a camera, a website, and shoe-store errands
bought
The answer
strangers will buy shoes they can't try on — proven with their own money
Source
Swinmurn founded Zappos (as Shoesite.com) in 1999; sold to Amazon in 2009 for $1.2B. The store-photo test is the canonical "Wizard of Oz" MVP — manual labor behind a storefront that looks automated. Storefront shown is a recreation, not the original page.

DoorDash, 2013 — "restaurants need delivery."

Four Stanford students hear a macaroon shop owner say she has to turn away delivery requests. They believe restaurant delivery is a business. What do they build?

One afternoon of work: a landing page called PaloAltoDelivery.com — PDF menus found on the internet, and their own cell number at the bottom. They went back to their dorm rooms. The phone rang within hours: shrimp pad thai and egg rolls. They delivered it themselves.

The plan, if anyone called, was to say "this is just a test — we're gathering data." Then a real order came in from a real stranger, and they went and got the food. The test converted them.

Source
Launched January 2013; first delivery January 12; $120K from Y Combinator that summer; renamed DoorDash. Founders did every delivery themselves for months — and DoorDash employees still do deliveries today.

Stripe, 2010 — "payments should be seven lines of code."

The Collison brothers believe developers hate their payment processors enough to switch. The product genuinely is a few lines of code. So how do they get their first users?

When a founder said yes to trying the beta, they didn't send a link. They said "Right then, give me your laptop" — and installed it on the spot, watching every hesitation, every broken assumption, every question the user actually asked.

The link-senders learn whether people say they'll try it. The Collisons learned what happens when they actually do — which is a different answer entirely.

Act II · What They Actually Built · The Room's Own Case

Franklin's Bagels — the $210,000 question.

Two weeks ago you priced this company. Year 3 of the model carries $210K of capex for a second location — and the whole valuation moved when it did. That number is a bet that a neighborhood Franklin's has never sold a bagel in will show up.

How do you de-risk $210,000 for $2,000?

A folding table. A farmers-market stall, six weekends, in the actual target neighborhood. Same bagels, same prices. You learn foot traffic, repeat faces by week three, what sells out, and what a Saturday grosses — before anyone signs a lease.

The lease teaches you the same lesson for $210,000 — after you've signed it. The stall teaches it for $2,000, first. No code anywhere in this MVP.

What's the MVP when you sell hours?

Move 1
The pilot engagement

Paid, scoped, small, and honest about being a pilot. A real client paying a real invoice is worth fifty "we'd definitely buy that" conversations.

Move 2
The concierge test

Deliver by hand what you'd eventually systematize. Zappos in a blazer: the client sees a service; you see whether the process is worth building.

Move 3
Sell before you staff

A signed LOI for an offering that doesn't exist yet is the service world's preorder. If nobody signs, you just saved a hire.

All three test the same thing: Question 4 — who's willing to pay? Before you build the team, the system, or the office to deliver it.

Act III · Deciding What Goes In First

The loop everyone quotes — and the part they skip.

The loop (Eric Ries, The Lean Startup): build the smallest thing that tests a hypothesis, measure what real customers do, learn whether the hypothesis survived — then go around again.

Measure the right thing. Vanity metrics go up and to the right and decide nothing: signups, page views, followers. Actionable metrics are behaviors that cost the customer something: repeat use, referrals, money.

The honest caveat: the loop assumes you already know what to measure — and which feature to test first. It doesn't tell you. That's the next fifteen minutes.

Act III · Deciding What Goes In First

Stated importance is free.
Tradeoffs tell the truth.

You already know this

S02: ask about past behavior, not future intentions. "Would you use this?" is worthless; "tell me about the last time" is evidence. Same rule, new domain.

The fix

Never ask what matters. Force a choice between whole packages at real prices — and read what they gave up. There's a sixty-year-old science for this — invented at Wharton, a mile up Walnut Street.

Act III · Deciding What Goes In First

Conjoint analysis — invented at Wharton.

Definition

Conjoint analysis is a survey method that measures what people actually value — by making them choose between complete packages instead of rating features one at a time.

The trick: it never asks what's important. It shows people complete packages — features bundled with a price — and makes them choose. Preference is inferred from what they were willing to give up, which is the only honest signal a hypothetical question can produce.

The pedigree

Developed by Paul Green in Wharton's marketing department in the mid-1960s; the landmark paper landed in 1971. It made him the most-cited author in marketing.

You've been in one

Conjoint designed Courtyard by Marriott and tested demand for E-ZPass. If you've driven the Schuylkill with a transponder, you're living inside a Wharton conjoint study.

Source
Green & Rao, "Conjoint Measurement for Quantifying Judgmental Data," Journal of Marketing Research, 1971. Marriott and E-ZPass applications per Wharton Magazine and Knowledge at Wharton.

Act IV · When Building Gets Cheap

Lean Startup rests on a premise. Check the date.

The Lean Startup is a 2011 book. Its core logic: building software is slow and expensive — months of engineering per attempt — so build the smallest thing that can teach you anything. The MVP was, at heart, a cost-control device.

2011
A landing page + working prototype
weeks of engineering, tens of thousands of dollars
→
2026
The same artifact
one prompt, a few minutes, effectively free

The premise collapsed. So does the discipline collapse with it?
Let's find out — live.

Act IV · When Building Gets Cheap

What we just did — and what we didn't.

What we have

A real page. Real copy, a real form, a real price on a real offering. Ten minutes ago it didn't exist. Cost: one prompt and ten minutes.

What we know

Nothing. Zero information about whether anyone wants a $2,500 audit. The page has taught us exactly as much as the unbuilt page: it hasn't met a customer yet.

The artifact was never the experiment. The experiment starts when a real prospect sees the page and decides. Everything before that moment is setup — and setup is now free. The customer's decision never will be.

Act IV · When Building Gets Cheap
Act IV · When Building Gets Cheap

The scarce resource was never engineering hours.

It's customer attention. You get a finite number of asks — emails answered, calls taken, pilots agreed to — before people stop replying. Spend them on a full product you guessed at, and you've burned the audience along with the build. That's what an MVP conserves now.

So spend your first asks where the signal is richest — early adopters:

Trait 1
Already in the problem

They're actively hacking together a solution today — which means they'll actually engage.

Trait 2
Unoffended by rough edges

Rough edges don't put them off — getting it first is the appeal.

Trait 3
Sought out by their circles

Known for expertise, asked for advice — they're where word of mouth starts.

The rest of the adoption curve — and the chasm between these people and everyone else — is Session 9.

How do you know it's working? One question.

"How would you feel if you could

Very disappointed Somewhat disappointed Not disappointed

40%+

the product-market-fit signal — keep going, start scaling
< 40%
"very disappointed"
keep iterating — growth spend on top of this leaks
Source
Sean Ellis — first marketer at Dropbox, LogMeIn, and Eventbrite — derived the 40% threshold by comparing survey results across roughly 100 startups (published ~2009). The wording matters: disappointment, not satisfaction — loss reveals need; praise is free. Calibration point: Slack scored 51% in an open 2015 survey.

The lesson

The honest read: yes — in one segment. The 41% clears the bar but n = 22 is thin. The tell is the shape of the curves: a retention curve that flattens is customers deciding to stay — that's fit. One that keeps decaying is a leaky bucket. Meridian has fit with 10–50-person firms and doesn't with enterprise. The move isn't "keep going" — it's focus.

Act V · Knowing When You Have It

Three signals that lie to you.

False positive 1
Friends & warm intros

They're buying you, not the offering. Real signal starts with strangers — or at least friends' referrals, one step removed.

False positive 2
Launch-day spikes

Novelty traffic decays by itself. Week-eight behavior is the truth; day-one behavior is a press release.

False positive 3
Paid traffic

Money can simulate demand indefinitely. If growth stops when spend stops, you rented the signal — you don't have it.

And one honest sentence on pivots: a pivot isn't giving up — it's a change of strategy that preserves what you learned. Which is only possible if you ran real experiments. Founder A from slide 3 can't pivot; she can only start over.

Act VI · Run It Like a Scientist

Hypothesis Testing and Results Analysis

We believe [customer] will [behavior].
We'll know within [timeframe] if [measurable threshold].

Fix this one — out loud

"Clients want more strategic guidance."

Can this sentence lose? Then it can't win either. Rebuild it in the format above.

One rebuild that can fail

"We believe owners of 10–50-person firms will pay $500 for a quarterly strategy session. We'll know within 3 weeks if 3 of the next 10 clients we offer it to buy."

One experiment at a time. Change two variables and you learn nothing about either.

Act VI · Run It Like a Scientist
Act VI · Run It Like a Scientist

A/B testing — honestly, at your scale.

The principle

One variable at a time, threshold set before you look.

The honest caveat

Classic A/B is a traffic game — significance takes volume a thirty-client firm will never have. Run the low-volume versions:

Low-volume move 1 · Sequential testing

The next ten proposals go out as version B — new structure, new pricing page, new opener. Compare close rates against the last ten. Not lab-grade, but decision-grade.

Low-volume move 2 · Test what you already ship

You don't have web traffic — you have outreach emails, proposals, pricing pages. Those are your funnel. Your proposal template is your homepage. Version it like one.

The discipline transfers even where the statistics can't:
decide what winning looks like before you peek.

The Experiment Card — five fields, no escape hatches.

1
Riskiest assumption

The belief that, if wrong, kills the idea. Usually a Question 4 answer.

2
What result proves you wrong

Written as a result you could actually observe — not a feeling.

3
Cheapest test that produces it

Stall, pilot, page, LOI — the least you can do that forces a real decision.

4
Threshold — set before you run

The number that means proceed, written down before the data arrives — so you can't move the goalposts after.

5
Deadline

Experiments without deadlines are hobbies.

Look again — it's the hypothesis format with a calendar attached. The card link comes to you after tonight.

The Prompt Pack — three interviews, chained.

P1
Foundation Interview

What it does: Claude interviews you, one question at a time, pushing each answer to the Four Foundation Questions until it names a specific customer, a specific problem, and what that problem costs today. Vague answers get challenged, not accepted.
