Act I · The Price of Money · Participation

Meet the players.

Has money now
Money Sellers

Savers, lenders, bondholders, LPs. They hand money over today and want more of it back later. Their question: what do you pay me to wait — and to risk not getting it back?

Needs money now
Money Buyers

Borrowers, founders, home-buyers, CFOs. They need money today and will pay for the privilege — in interest, or in a piece of the company. Their question: what's the cheapest way to rent or raise it?

Wants the outcome
Speculators

Traders and speculators. They don't need the money and aren't lending it — they're buying and selling the risk itself, betting on which way a price moves. Their question: what does the crowd have wrong?

Which one are you? Trick question — most of you were all three before lunch. A 401(k) makes you a seller. A mortgage makes you a buyer. The March Madness bracket makes you a gambler. Tonight's skill: knowing which seat you're in, and what the person across the table wants.

Act II · The Price of Time
Act II · The Time Value of Money

Calculating value now and in the future.

Future Value (FV) — what money today becomes later

FV = PV × (1 + r)n

Money moving forward through time grows at rate r for n years. $10,000 today at 10% is worth $11,000 in a year — and $16,105 in five, because each year's growth earns its own growth. That's compounding.

Present Value (PV) — what money promised later is worth today

PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n

Money moving backward shrinks. A promise of $11,000 next year, at that same 10%, is worth exactly $10,000 today. Same formula, run in reverse. Finance calls this discounting, and r is the discount rate.

Everything tonight runs on these two formulas. Valuing a company, pricing a loan, judging a term sheet — all of it is pulling future dollars back to today. The only argument, ever, is about r: the rate. So where does r come from?

β ("beta") measures how risky an investment is compared to the market as a whole. The riskier the bet, the higher the β — and the higher the return investors demand. The line below is this equation, drawn left to right.

Act III · Pricing the Future
Act III · Pricing the Future · The Whole Idea

A DCF answers one question: what is a stream of future cash worth today?

NPV = nΣt = 1  CFt ÷ (1 + r)t  +  TV ÷ (1 + r)n

DCF — discounted cash flow: each year's cash flow (CF), pulled back to today with the PV formula, then added up. NPV — net present value: the total. Three parts, in order:

1

The cash flows

What the business will actually hand you, year by year. You just spent two sessions learning to find this number — it's the cash flow statement, projected forward.

2

The discount rate

The price of waiting plus the price of risk — picked off the spectrum you just saw. Each year's cash gets pulled back to today with the PV formula — divided by (1+r), once per year of waiting.

3

The terminal value

You can't project forever. At some point you stop — and pretend you sell the whole thing. The sale price at the end is the third part.

The next six slides walk this picture left to right: the flows, the rate, the pull-back, the sale, the answer — then the same math run in reverse.

Act III · Part ③ · The Terminal Value

At Year 5, we stop projecting — and sell the shop.

Nobody can see Year 12. So the model ends with a pretend sale: hand the business to a buyer at the end of Year 5. What would a buyer pay? In the real world, buyers quote multiples — a price expressed as "how many years of X."

Mature & steady → price the profits

P / E — price to earnings

"I'll pay five years' worth of what it earns." For businesses whose story is the cash they make now.

Young & growing → price the scale

P / S — price to sales

"I'll pay for the revenue and the trajectory." For businesses that are all growth and no profit — yet. (VCs live here.)

5 × $290K Year-5 cash flow = $1.45M sale price  →  $1.45M ÷ (1.20)5 = ≈ $583K today

Calculating the terminal value using a P/E ratio of 5.0 against Year-5 cash flow of $290,000 yields a sale price of $1.45M. However, that $1.45M arrives five years from now — so we discount it for time: the present value is $583,000. The 5.0 ratio is our assumption, not a market quote. We test it in the model.

Act III · The Same Math, Reversed

Calculating investment return.

IRR — Internal Rate of Return

The annual return a stream of cash flows actually earns on the money invested. Mathematically: the discount rate at which NPV equals exactly zero — the rate where the deal breaks even.

Two questions, one formula

NPV asks: given my rate, what are these cash flows worth? We just answered it — r = 20% → $917K.
IRR asks: given the price, what do these cash flows pay? You pick the dollars; the formula returns a rate.

Franklin asks $700K for the whole business today  →  his projected stream pays 27.7% per year

Now the two answers can talk to each other: the stream pays 27.7%, and we demanded 20% — so at $700K, this deal clears our bar. This is the language investors speak. A VC won't say "your NPV is positive at my rate." They'll say "we underwrite to a 30% IRR" — meaning: at the price we're offering, the stream had better pay 30%.

Where the numbers come from

Same five cash flows + the $1.45M sale, against a $700K price today. Solve for the rate that balances the scale: 27.7%. (Excel does the solving — you'll see the formula in a minute.)

Act IV · Debt vs Equity

Debt is renting money. Equity is selling the machine that makes it.

Buyer B just showed you why debt is cheaper for the buyer and the seller of money. The bank took the safe seat — first in line, house as collateral, 6% and capped there. B kept every dollar of upside past the interest. Equity flips both seats: the investor takes the risk seat, and charges accordingly.

Act IV · Debt vs Equity
Act IV · The Blend

Most companies drink both. Their cost of money is the blend.

Cost of capital = (cost of debt × share of debt) + (cost of equity × share of equity)

Weight each source's cost by how much of it you use. Finance calls the blend WACC — the weighted average cost of capital.

Why it matters

The blend is the hurdle every project inside a company must clear. Money that costs 12% can't fund projects that pay 8%.

Why debt lowers the blend

Debt is the cheaper source — lenders sit in the safer seat, and interest is tax-deductible. Up to the point where the payments can kill you.

Where each cost comes from

Cost of debt is easy to see — it's the interest rate on the loan. Cost of equity is the return investors require for the risk — the β equation from Act II prices it. The full build lands in S07, where the blend becomes the discount rate for your pro forma.

Act IV · The Third Lever

Understanding CFO magic.

ROE = Net incomeRevenue × RevenueAssets × AssetsEquity

The DuPont breakdown of return on equity. Notice Revenue and Assets each cancel out — the three fractions multiply down to Net income ÷ Equity, the return owners actually earn.

Profit margin

Net income ÷ Revenue — how much of each sale you keep. Raise prices, cut costs. An operating skill.

Asset turnover

Revenue ÷ Assets — how much revenue each dollar of assets produces. An operating skill.

Leverage

Assets ÷ Equity — how much of the asset base is funded with borrowed money. A financing choice.

Two of these levers are operating skills — earn more per sale, get more sales from the same assets. The third is a financing choice: run the business on borrowed money. Leverage multiplies the result of the other two — in both directions. A spectacular ROE deserves one question first: which lever produced it?

Act V · How Risk Gets Priced
Act V · How Risk Gets Priced

Six criteria impacting your cost of money.

Revenue Quality

Subscription revenue reads safe. Hit-driven, lumpy, one-big-customer revenue reads risky.

Industry Volatility

People buy bagels in recessions. They don't buy yachts. Utilities sit left on the line; crypto exchanges sit far right.

GDP Correlation

If your sales rise and fall with the economy, investors can't hide from you in a downturn — they charge for that.

Debt Levels

Act IV's lesson: leverage amplifies everything — including how risky your equity is to hold.

Liquidity

A public stock sells in seconds. A bagel-shop stake sells in months, at a discount, maybe. Illiquidity costs extra.

Exit Date

Cash next quarter vs. cash in Year 9. The further out the payoff, the more heavily it gets discounted.

Wall Street compresses all six into the single number β from Act II. You don't need to compute it — you need the criteria. They're what an investor is silently scoring while you pitch, and every answer moves your rate.

Act VI · Exotic Financial Instruments
Act VI · Exotic Instruments · Options

You've bought options before. They were just called other things.

A put is an insurance policy

You pay a premium. If disaster hits — the stock craters — you still get to sell at the price the policy guarantees. If nothing happens, the premium is gone and you're glad. Homeowner's insurance, for a share of stock.

A call is a deposit that reserves a price

You pay a small fee to lock the right to buy at today's agreed price. If it soars, you buy at the locked price and smile. If it doesn't, you walk away, out only the fee. Putting a deposit on a house you're not sure about yet.

Notice what's actually for sale in both: not the stock — the risk. The put seller is paid to carry your downside. The call seller is paid to hand over your upside. Once you see risk itself changing hands, every instrument in this act makes sense.

Act VI · Exotic Instruments · One Story

Options in use.

Marcus took his AI-powered SEO company public on NASDAQ in March. He owns a million shares at $32 — $32 million on paper. The IPO lockup says he can't sell a single share for six months. Every morning he watches a number that is his entire net worth, and he is not allowed to do anything about it.

Buys puts at $25
The insurance
whatever happens, he can sell at $25 — worst case locked at $25M
paid for by
Selling calls at $45
The upside, sold
if it rockets past $45, the buyer of those calls gets the ride — not Marcus

The call premium pays for the put premium — the trade costs him nothing out of pocket. His six months are now bounded: never worse than $25M, never better than $45M. He gave up the upside above $45 to eliminate the risk below $25 — at no cost. The structure is called a collar.
