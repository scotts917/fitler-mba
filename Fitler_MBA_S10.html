Michael Porter, Harvard Business School · Competitive Strategy, 1980 · "What Is Strategy?" HBR, 1996

Porter

Four minutes. Who he is: Harvard Business School professor. His 1979 article and 1980 book became the backbone of strategy teaching for forty years. Every consulting firm and business school still starts here.

The big idea in plain words: before you make a single decision, the industry you're in has already decided a lot about how much money you can make. Some industries are rich, some are poor, and it's mostly structure.

Walk the four rows. Don't teach them yet. Say: we'll take each of these in turn in a few minutes.

Why it's a little dated: it treats the industry like a photograph. It assumes the boundaries hold still. The next two books are reactions to that.

W. Chan Kim & Renée Mauborgne, INSEAD · Blue Ocean Strategy, 2005

Blue Ocean

Four minutes. Who they are: two professors at INSEAD, the business school outside Paris. The book came out in 2005 and sold millions of copies.

The picture: a red ocean is bloody with sharks fighting over the same fish. A blue ocean is open water nobody's fishing yet.

The direct argument with Porter is row two. Porter said pick cheaper or better. Kim and Mauborgne say you can do both if you stop spending on things customers don't care about. They call it value innovation.

Row four is the one founders miss. Everyone studies their customers. Almost nobody studies the people who don't buy anything in the category.

Honest caveat: they picked cases that worked, and blue oceans turn red once people see them.

Rita Gunther McGrath, Columbia Business School · The End of Competitive Advantage, 2013

McGrath

Four minutes. Who she is: Columbia Business School professor. Her 2013 book argues that "sustainable competitive advantage," the thing Porter taught everyone to chase, is now the exception.

The big idea: in a world that moves this fast, any edge you have is temporary. She calls it transient advantage. The real skill is building the next advantage while the current one still pays.

This isn't Porter being wrong. His tools still tell you whether a spot makes money. McGrath says don't expect to stay there.

Row three matters most for this room: companies that adapt well don't change everything. They keep their identity and relationships steady and keep changing the offer.

Part 2 · Going deeper · Porter

Five forces decide how much money an industry leaves on the table.

New entrants
How easy is it for someone new to start?
Strong when it takes little money, skill or licensing to open up.
Suppliers
Can the people you buy from squeeze you?
Strong when there are only a few of them and switching is hard.
Rivalry
How hard do competitors fight each other?
Strong when there are many look-alikes, high fixed costs and slow growth.
Buyers
Can your customers squeeze you?
Strong when they're big, few, or can compare and switch easily.
Substitutes
Can the customer solve the problem another way?
Strong when the other way is cheaper or good enough.

Strong forces take the profit. Weak forces leave it for you. The strongest one sets the ceiling.

The point

Find where the forces are weakest. Or change one.

Michael Porter, Harvard Business School · "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy," HBR 1979 · Competitive Strategy, 1980

Five Forces

Five minutes. First row from the Porter page, now in full.

His big idea: the industry you're in decides how much profit is available before you make a single decision. Some industries are rich, some are poor, and it's mostly structure.

Walk the five in plain words. Each one is a question about who has the power to take money away from you.

Optional example the room will get: residential real estate. Entrants: the license is easy, so lots of agents. Buyers: people compare agents and push on commission. Substitutes: sell-it-yourself sites and discount brokers. Rivalry: many agents, all offering roughly the same thing. That's why the top agents compete on reputation and referrals, not price.

Government, technology and growth aren't forces. They act through the five.

Critique in one line, save the rest for McGrath: it's a snapshot. It assumes the industry holds still.

Part 2 · Porter · What Is Strategy?

Being better isn't a strategy. Being different is.

Southwest · the original low-fare airline

Doing the same things better gets copied. Southwest chose different things, and each choice makes the others work. A rival can copy one piece. Copying the whole system means giving up what they already are.

Continental Lite · 1993 to 1995

Copied the pieces. Kept the full-service airline around them. Gone in two years.

The test

What do you refuse to do for customers? If the answer is nothing, you don't have a strategy yet.

Porter, "What Is Strategy?" HBR, Nov–Dec 1996

Being better isn't a strategy

Five minutes. This is the most useful Porter for founders.

One line from the Porter page first: three ways to win are lowest cost, different enough to charge more, or focused on one group. Try to be all three and you're stuck in the middle: not cheap enough for the price shopper, not special enough for the premium buyer. Story slot: Vanguard, cost leadership built into the structure. You launched their online division, so tell it from the inside. Then Southwest.

Doing the same things better (operational effectiveness) is not strategy. Best practices spread, everyone converges, margins get competed away. Strategy is doing different things, or the same things differently.

It takes tradeoffs. The strategy is in what you choose not to do. Southwest said no to meals, seat assignments, bag transfers and big hubs. Each "no" made the fares and the turns possible.

Fit is the moat. That's why nobody copied Southwest for decades.

Continental Lite, told live: Continental tried to be Southwest inside a full-service airline. Same fares, kept the hubs, the travel agents, the frequent-flyer program. It couldn't make the tradeoffs and was folded back in within two years.

AI note for later: when everyone gets the same tools, doing it better gets even easier to copy. Positioning matters more, not less.

Hold this: Southwest shows up again on the wave slide.

W. Chan Kim & Renée Mauborgne, INSEAD · Blue Ocean Strategy, 2005

Red vs. blue

Three minutes. First row from the Blue Ocean page, side by side.

The picture: a red ocean is bloody with competitors fighting over the same fish. A blue ocean is open water nobody's fishing yet.

The fight with Porter is the last row. Porter said pick cheaper or better, and if you try both you get stuck in the middle. Kim and Mauborgne call doing both "value innovation": cut the things the industry spends on that customers don't care about, and use the savings to add something new. The next slide shows how.

Their other big idea: look at noncustomers. People who don't buy from anyone in your industry today. That's where new demand hides.

Participation: hands for red, hands for blue. Most of the room will be red. That's normal. The question is whether one part of what they do could go blue.

Porter said

Pick one. Blue Ocean says the grid lets you do both.

Kim & Mauborgne, Blue Ocean Strategy, 2005 · Cirque du Soleil founded 1984

The circus

Three minutes. Reveal, don't build live. Save the live building for Éclat.

Set it up in thirty seconds: early 1980s, the circus is losing its audience. Kids want video games. Animal-rights pressure is rising. Star performers hold the owners hostage on pay. Ask once: you just bought a circus, what would you cut? Take one or two answers, then reveal the grid box by box.

The idea: red oceans are existing markets fighting over existing customers. Blue oceans are new market space. Eliminate and Reduce take cost out. Raise and Create add value. That's "value innovation," and it's a direct shot at Porter's "stuck in the middle."

Honest caveat: Cirque went into creditor protection in 2020 when COVID shut every show. Even a blue ocean needs defending.

Second story, if the room is with you: [yellow tail]. A cheap Australian wine, US launch 2001. It dropped the wine jargon, the aging talk and the big ad budgets, and made wine easy to drink, easy to pick off the shelf, and fun. It won beer and cocktail drinkers who found wine intimidating. That's the noncustomer idea from the last slide. Don't quote sales volumes; sources disagree badly.

Services version: QB House in Japan. Ten-minute haircut, no shampoo, no appointment.

The warning, and the bridge to McGrath: blue oceans turn red. Curves found women who avoided gyms, grew very fast, then copycats arrived. Once it works, everyone can see it.

Stable core, fast edges. Who you are, your culture and your customer relationships stay put. What you sell and how you sell it keep moving.

Your business

Which column describes your world right now?

Rita Gunther McGrath, Columbia Business School · The End of Competitive Advantage, 2013

Stable vs. changing

Three minutes. This is the core of McGrath side by side with Porter's world.

The distinction: in a stable world you find a good position and defend it for decades. In a changing world every advantage wears out, so the real skill is building the next one while this one still pays. She calls it transient advantage: an edge that's temporary by nature.

This isn't Porter being wrong. His tools still tell you whether a position makes money. McGrath says don't expect it to last.

Stable core, fast edges is the part most summaries miss. Her standout companies didn't change everything all the time. They held identity, culture and relationships steady, and kept moving the offers.

Participation: which column? Most service businesses in this room will say "changing," especially since AI showed up.

Arenas

Three minutes. Participation beat. Reveal "the phone" one column at a time. By the third one the room is laughing. That's the point.

McGrath calls these arenas: competition defined by the customer's job, not the product category. Threats come from outside your industry, where you're not looking.

AI example: Chegg sold homework help. Starting in 2023, students went to ChatGPT instead. Chegg didn't lose to another homework company. Keep stock numbers out of it.

Take two answers to the question. Push for at least one competitor outside their industry.

Part 2 · McGrath · Transient advantage

Every advantage wears out. Know where you are on the wave.

Most companies are built for one stage: cashing in. It's profitable and it feels safe, so they stay too long.

Your business

Where is your main offer on this curve? What's in launch behind it?

McGrath, The End of Competitive Advantage, 2013 · Kodak Chapter 11 filed Jan 19, 2012 · Fujifilm launched Astalift skincare in 2007

The wave

Five minutes. Participation beat. Before revealing Kodak and Fujifilm, ask: name a company that was the best at what it did ten years ago and isn't now. What happened? Place two or three of their answers on the curve.

Kodak and Fujifilm: same threat, opposite responses. A Kodak engineer, Steve Sasson, built the first hand-held, self-contained digital camera in 1975. Kodak kept defending film. Fujifilm took what it knew about chemistry and film coatings into skincare, medical imaging and materials for flat-screen displays.

Blockbuster: profitable in stores until it wasn't. That's the exploitation trap.

Netflix: DVDs by mail, then streaming, then originals. It ate its own business on purpose. The 2011 Qwikster split shows the transitions are hard even when you do them.

Southwest callback: the system from the Porter section lasted fifty years. Southwest started charging for bags in May 2025 and flew its first assigned-seat flights in January 2026. Even the best-fitting system gets reconfigured.

How you know the wave is breaking (McGrath's warning signs, say them out loud): each new idea is smaller than the last. Customers find something cheaper that's good enough. Competitors show up from places you didn't expect. Your best people leave. Discounting creeps in. Growth only comes from cost cuts or acquisitions. Nobody inside is excited anymore.

Participation: "What are you still doing that you should walk away from?" Take two answers.

Careers are waves too. For this room that lands hard. Say it and let it sit.

Putting it together

Four minutes. Participation beat.

Walk the three rows fast. They stack. Porter tells you whether a position makes money while it lasts. Blue Ocean helps you find the next one. McGrath says assume it won't last.

The AI row: when everyone has the same tools, doing things better stops being an edge. That pushes advantage back to position, proprietary data, and how your pieces fit. AI also makes the Raise and Create boxes cheap, especially in services: personal attention, expertise on demand, instant response. And it shortens the wave. What took competitors years to copy now takes weeks.

Poll by hands. Take two volunteers on why.

What still holds up: what you know that others don't, how fast you learn, and fit.
